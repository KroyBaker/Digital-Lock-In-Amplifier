December 30th, 2025

I decided to use the LTC2208 as my Analog Digital Converter (ADC). Its 130 Msps sampling rate and 100 dB SFDR (Spurious-Free Dynamic Range) are ideal for the DLIA, which must resolve signals 60 dB to 80 dB (1,000x to 10,000x) below the noise floor

For the ADC, we still need a way to make sure it only picks up specific frequencys. This is where the Analog Front-End (AFE) is used.

The AFE for this design comprises a Transimpedance Amplifier (TIA), an anti-aliasing filter, and a differential ADC driver. The TIA first converts the input signal current into a proportional voltage. This voltage is then processed by a 4th-order Butterworth low-pass filter. I selected the Butterworth topology for its maximally flat passband response, which ensures signal integrity, while the 4th-order design provides a sweet spot, offering a steep enough roll-off to suppress noise without introducing excessive group delay.
With a system sampling rate of 130 Msps, the Nyquist limit is 65 MHz. However, I decided to set the filter’s cutoff frequency at 50 MHz to provide a sufficient transition band; this ensures that frequencies near the Nyquist limit are adequately attenuated before sampling. Finally, the signal is passed through a 50 Ω matching resistor to the differential ADC driver (Exact model not yet chosen), which prepares the signal for the LTC2208 by providing the required balanced format and common-mode biasing.

Designing the filter is easy, the difficult part is choosing which exact resistors, capacitors, and inductors to use.
Currently, I am aiming to find extremely precise and sturdy parts which will be able to work well under a fair amount of heat.


January 1st, 2026

For the design, I decided to add a LT3042 and LT3094 which will act as the voltage linear regulators for the positive and negative rails of the Transimpedance Amplifier. I chose those two as they seemed simple enough to use for someone new to designing electronics and also provided ultralow noise, which is needed to make sure it doesn't interfere with the signal.
Additionally, I added the photodiode which I connected to the V- pin of the Transimpedance Amplifier

After lots of careful consideration, for the Low Pass Filter (LPF), I picked out 2 50 ohm resistors, a 300nH and 120nH inductor, a 47pF and 120pF capacitor. For the Transinpedence Amplifer, I chose a 10kOhm resistor and 0.1pF capacitor. 
For the Positive LDO Regulator and Negative LDO Regulator, I chose a 24.9kOhm resistor, a 4.7uF capactior, a polarized 10uF capacitor, and 0.1uF capacitor respectively.

The LPF could use many different values for the circuit, but I chose to build it with those exact components as they are easier to find exact parts for those values. 

For the Transinpedence Amplifer, we connect the V- pin to the FB 1 pin using the parallel combination of the 10kOhm resistor and 0.1pF capacitor because it creates the gain necessary to turn the small current from the photodiode into something much larger. The 10kOhm Resistor was chosen due to bandwidth and noise. The 10kOhm Resistor gives enough gain for the average nanophotonics signal and gives approximately 159MHz bandwidth, which allows us to take full advantage of the 130 MHz signal from the ADC. The OPA855 is a "decompensated" amplifier, meaning it is naturally prone to oscillating at very high frequencies. This is why I chose to add a 0.1 pF capacitor, this give the signal its own built in low pass filter. The exact value came from the official datasheet.

Finally, the LT3094 & LT3045 uses a 4.7 μF + 24.9 kΩ combination connected to the set pin. The 24.9kΩ is used because the LT3094 & LT3045 produce around 100 μA from the set pin. Because the +Vin and -Vin rails need around 2.5 Volts, dividing 2.5/100 μA gives us 25kΩs. I made it slightly less as to account for error and slight deviations from 100μA. The 4.7 μF as it helps with reducing noise generated by the internal reference current source directly to ground 

The Output network uses a 10 μF and a 0.1 μF capacitor. I used a Tantalum Capacitor for the 10μF one and a Multilayer Ceramic Capacitor for the 0.1 μF one. The Ceramic Capacitor helps shunt very high frequencies while the Tantalum Capacitor shunts lower to mid range frequencies. The combination of both of them creates a band pass filter.

One issue I have with the design is the 10kΩ resistor which will be connected to the feedback loop. Although it is good for 100 μA, anything below 1 μA is not large enough to measure and anything higher than 250 μA will clip the current.
